# Written description for Tableau visualization

> What software did you use to create your data visualization? 

I created this visualization using Tableau Public, a web-based data visualization platform designed for exploratory and explanatory analysis. Tableau Public was selected for its ability to efficiently produce ranked comparative visualizations and for its accessibility to broad audiences through interactive, web-based dissemination. Its drag-and-drop interface also supported rapid iteration during the analytical process.


> Who is your intended audience? 

The intended audience includes members of the general public, students, journalists, and policy stakeholders who are interested in understanding geographic disparities in crime severity across Canada. The visualization is designed for audiences without technical or statistical training, relying on intuitive ranking, clear labels, and visual ordering rather than complex analytical notation.


> What information or message are you trying to convey with your visualization? 

The visualization communicates the concentration and uneven distribution of crime severity across Canadian geographies in 2008, as measured by the Crime Severity Index (CSI). By ranking provinces, territories, and selected sub-provincial regions, the chart highlights the striking disparity between Canada’s northern territories, particularly the Northwest Territories and Nunavut, and the rest of the country. The message emphasizes that crime severity is not evenly distributed and is geographically concentrated, with northern regions experiencing substantially higher CSI values than southern provinces and urban areas.


> What aspects of design did you consider when making your visualization? How did you apply them? With what elements of your plots? 

Several core design principles informed the visualization. A horizontal bar chart was chosen to support clear ranking and ease of comparison across many geographic categories. Bars are ordered from highest to lowest CSI to guide visual attention and reduce cognitive load. Colour intensity reinforces magnitude, while numeric labels provide precise values without requiring estimation from axes alone. The x-axis begins at zero to preserve proportional accuracy, and unnecessary gridlines and decorative elements were minimized to maintain focus on the data.


> How did you ensure that your data visualizations are reproducible? If the tool you used to make your data visualization is not reproducible, how will this impact your data visualization? 

Reproducibility was supported through the consistent use of a cleaned and documented dataset and transparent transformations performed within Tableau, including filtering, aggregation, and sorting. However, because Tableau Public embeds data transformations within the workbook rather than external scripts, exact reproduction depends on access to the original Tableau file. This reflects a trade-off between reproducibility and accessibility typical of GUI-based visualization tools.


> How did you ensure that your data visualization is accessible? 

Accessibility was addressed by using a high-contrast colour scale, clear text labels, and sufficient spacing between bars to support readability. The ranked structure allows viewers to interpret the chart without relying solely on colour, and numeric annotations provide redundancy. Being web-based, the visualization also supports browser-level zoom and accessibility tools, though Tableau Public has known limitations for screen-reader users.


> Who are the individuals and communities who might be impacted by your visualization? 

The visualization represents communities across Canada, particularly those in northern and remote regions that experience disproportionately high crime severity. These communities may be affected by how the data are interpreted in public and policy discourse. As such, the visualization focuses on structural and geographic patterns rather than attributing responsibility or blame to specific populations.


> How did you choose which features of your chosen dataset to include or exclude from your visualization? 

The visualization includes geographies that allow for meaningful comparison across territorial, provincial, and selected sub-provincial levels. While this introduces differences in scale and population size, inclusion was intentional to foreground inequality that may be obscured by national or provincial averages alone. Features not directly relevant to comparative crime severity were excluded to maintain analytical clarity.


> What ‘underwater labour’ contributed to your final data visualization product?

Significant “underwater labour” supported the final visualization, including cleaning inconsistent geographic labels, resolving formatting issues, validating CSI values, selecting an appropriate aggregation level, and making deliberate decisions about ranking and inclusion. Additional effort was required to balance analytical rigor with communicative clarity for a non-technical audience.
